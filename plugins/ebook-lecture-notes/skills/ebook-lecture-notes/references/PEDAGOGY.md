## 1. The failure mode

Compressed notes fail in a specific, predictable way. They become **accurate and dead.**

Every fact is right. Nothing is remembered. The reader gets through page three and their eyes are moving but nothing is going in. They close the file and never open it again — which means the compression achieved nothing, because unread 15 pages and unread 300 pages are worth exactly the same.

This is not a formatting problem. You cannot fix it with bullet points, headers, or bold text. Those are the things people reach for when the writing is boring, and they make it worse — a wall of bullets is harder to read than a wall of prose, not easier.

The fix is structural: **make the reader do something on every page.** Answer a question, notice a contradiction, feel surprised, disagree. A reader who is only receiving is a reader who is leaving.

## 2. Six rules of the lecturer voice

### Rule 1 — Open with the puzzle, not the definition

The instinct is to define the concept and then illustrate it. Reverse it. Pose the problem, let the reader commit to an answer, *then* explain. A reader who has guessed wrong is now invested.

> **Dead:** Exponential growth is difficult for humans to intuitively grasp because our cognition is adapted to linear extrapolation.
>
> **Alive:** One bacterium in a glass at 11:00pm. It doubles every minute. The glass is full at midnight. **When is it half full?**
>
> Almost everyone says 11:30. The answer is 11:59.
>
> That gap — between 11:30 and 11:59 — is the entire reason debt crises feel like surprises.

The second version is longer. Use it anyway. Compression is about cutting *low-value* words, not all words.

### Rule 2 — Name things, and keep the author's names

A named framework is portable. An unnamed one evaporates.

If the author named it, keep their name — even a slightly awkward one. *"The leaking car"* beats *"the accumulating-maintenance-cost model"* because the reader can picture it, and because it's the handle they'll find again if they go back to the source.

If the author didn't name it, name it yourself, and say you did.

### Rule 3 — Specific numbers, never adjectives

> **Dead:** The Fed's balance sheet expanded dramatically in recent decades.
>
> **Alive:** $150 billion in 1980. $4.1 trillion in 2019. Then $6.7 trillion — in eleven weeks.

"Dramatically" asks the reader to take your word for it. The numbers make them feel it. And the third figure lands hardest because of the time unit, not the amount.

### Rule 4 — Every significant claim gets a counterargument

This is what separates lecture notes from a book report, and it's the rule most likely to get dropped under time pressure.

A student who memorizes a book has learned nothing transferable. A student who can state the strongest case against it has learned how to read. So after presenting a claim well, present its weakness — and present the weakness *seriously*, not as a token hedge.

Be especially suspicious of the book's most quotable claims. Memorability and rigor are uncorrelated; often inversely. The line that sounds most brilliant is usually where the author's rhetoric outran their evidence.

> **Token hedge (useless):** Of course, some economists disagree with this view.
>
> **Real counterargument:** Two ratios landing on the same round number is striking, and striking is exactly when to slow down. No mechanism is offered that would *force* them to match — and with different start or end dates, they wouldn't. This is where the book's rhetoric is strongest and its evidence thinnest.

### Rule 5 — Concrete before abstract, always

The general principle is what you want the reader to keep. The concrete instance is how it gets in.

Don't write "the author argues that emergency interventions compound." Write the college car with the oil leak, the transmission fluid, the taped hose, the row of bottles in the back seat and the five extra hours a week at McDonald's — and *then* say: this is the monetary system.

### Rule 6 — Vary the texture

Six pages of uniform paragraphs reads as slog regardless of quality. Alternate deliberately:

prose → table → a single bold line on its own → prose → chart → question → prose

A table is a rest. A one-line callout is emphasis. A question is a wake-up. Use them for rhythm, not decoration.

**But:** never let the structure exceed the content. Three bullets of four words each is a list pretending to be an idea. If it fits in a sentence, write the sentence.

## 3. Layer specs with worked examples

### Layer 1 — The one-page map

Must contain:
- **The logline.** If the author gave one, use theirs. Otherwise write one — the whole book in one or two sentences, the way you'd pitch a film.
- **The argument in 5–7 numbered steps.** Each step is a claim, not a topic. "The Fed was built to enrich its founders" is a claim. "The history of the Fed" is a topic and tells the reader nothing.
- **The one thing to remember.** A single sentence, set apart. Everything else can go; this survives.
- **Who is telling you this.** Author, credentials, and what they gain if you believe them.

Test: hand this page to someone who hasn't read the book. Can they hold a five-minute conversation about it? If not, the map failed.

### Layer 2 — Mental models

The highest-value layer. 4–8 models, each structured:

```
## [Name] (original-language name if applicable)

[The model — concrete, redrawable, ideally with the author's own illustration]

**The point:** [what it explains, in one line]

**Use it when:** [generalization beyond this book — this line is the payload]
```

The *"Use it when"* line is what makes this layer worth more than the book. The author applies each model to their own thesis only. Turning it into a portable tool is your contribution.

> **Example:**
>
> **The asymptote.** A 75-year-old with clogged arteries. His doctor says one more cigarette could kill him. **The heart attack is not caused by the last cigarette** — it's caused by forty years of accumulation.
>
> **The point:** the crisis moment is determined by the curve, not the trigger.
>
> **Use it when:** anyone blames a crisis on the proximate event. Ask what the curve was doing for the preceding decades.

### Layer 3 — The evidence

Tables over prose. Every number traced to text actually read. Group by argument, not by chapter.

Add a **calibration line** wherever it helps — a number is meaningless without a reference point. "CAPE of 32" means nothing; "CAPE of 32, against a historical average of 17 and a 1929 reading of 31" means everything.

### Layer 4 — Chapter index

One line each. Mark ★ for chapters carrying unique content, unmarked for restatement. **Say which ratings are verified and which are inferred from sampling.**

End with the verdict — which chapters carry the book. This is the most actionable line in the entire document for a reader deciding whether to open the original.

### Layer 5 — The critical layer

Structure as numbered points, each a specific challenge, not a general disclaimer.

Always include, in roughly this priority:
1. **Author's interest.** What do they gain if you believe this?
2. **Age.** What has happened since publication that tests these claims?
3. **The weakest load-bearing claim.** Not the weakest claim — the weakest one the argument *depends on*.
4. **Pattern-matching dressed as evidence.** Cycles fitted to two data points; post-hoc criteria that no test could have failed.
5. **Conspicuous absence.** What would a serious critic raise that the book never addresses?

Close with a verdict on the book — one or two sentences, direct. *"Strong diagnosis, weak proof, self-interested prescription."*

### Layer 6 — What was cut

List categories, not items. Then give search terms for the source, in the source's language. This is the contract that makes the compression honest.

### The self-test

5–7 questions. Mix recall ("name the five warning signs") with synthesis ("state the strongest argument against the central proposal"). At least one question must require the reader to argue against the book.

Frame it as diagnostic, not homework: whatever you can't answer is where the notes cut too deep — patch that section specifically rather than expanding everything.

## 4. Sentence-level patterns

**Cut these openings.** They add length and subtract force:
- "It is important to note that…"
- "The author argues that…" (just state the claim; attribute once at the top)
- "This chapter discusses…"
- "In conclusion…"

**Use second person for application, third for reporting.** "The author claims X" reports. "Use this when you see Y" applies. Mixing them deliberately keeps the reader oriented on which is which.

**One idea per paragraph.** If a paragraph has two, split it — the second one is being hidden.

**Bold for the load-bearing sentence in a section, and nowhere else.** Bold on every third phrase is the same as no bold.

**Write the number before the adjective.** "37×" then "understated," not "massively understated (37×)."

## 5. Self-check before delivering

Read your own output cold and ask:

1. Is there a question anywhere in the first page? If not, the reader is passive from the start.
2. Could a competent summarizer have produced this? If yes, the voice failed.
3. Does every mental model have a "Use it when"?
4. Does the critical layer name what the author gains?
5. Is there a paragraph you'd skip if you were reading this? Cut it — the reader will skip it too, but they'll lose confidence in the rest first.
6. Would you rather read these notes or the book? If the book, start over.
